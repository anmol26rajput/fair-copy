Smith, J. (2020). Sleep and Learning in Higher Education. Journal of Study Science, 12(3), 45-67. (Fictional sample source.)

Sleep plays a central role in the consolidation of newly acquired memories because the brain replays recent experiences during slow wave sleep and gradually transfers them from short term storage in the hippocampus to long term storage in the neocortex.

Students who sleep fewer than six hours before an examination consistently perform worse on tasks that require recalling facts, even when they have spent the same number of hours studying as their well rested peers.

The benefits of regular sleep are not limited to memory, since attention and mood also improve noticeably after a full night of rest, and these gains tend to build up over several weeks rather than appearing after a single night.

A well rested learner is therefore better prepared to absorb new material, connect it with prior knowledge, and apply it to unfamiliar problems during assessment.

Caffeine, by contrast, offers only a short lived boost and does not replace the restorative effects of sleep.
